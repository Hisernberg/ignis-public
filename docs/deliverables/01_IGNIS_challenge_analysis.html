Executive Summary

We scored all fourteen official challenges on data availability (30%), demonstration impact (25%), AI leverage under a chat-only constraint (20%), narrative strength (15%) and competition density (10%). Three challenges separated from the field: Harmonization of MODIS and VIIRS Hot Spots (winner, 8.9/10), Be An Earth System Trend Detective (8.1) and Dancing with the SARs (7.8). The winner is the only challenge where the team's exact credentials — a FIRMS MAP_KEY, an elevated api.nasa.gov key, Earthdata Login, GIBS and Hugging Face — cover 100% of the data path, and where a 25-year, scientifically meaningful web artifact can be built and deployed within hackathon limits. The flagship build IGNIS was constructed, verified and deployed during this planning cycle; this dossier documents why it will win.

01How We Scored All Fourteen

A weighted matrix tuned for one goal: maximize probability of a global win under our real constraints.

Challenge selection is the highest-leverage decision in Space Apps: a median idea on the right challenge beats a brilliant idea on the wrong one. We began from the official list of fourteen challenges published for 2026 and reduced each to its hard data path — what must be fetched, from where, at what freshness, and under which credential. We then scored every challenge on five factors whose weights reflect our strategy: data availability 0.30 (can we get authentic NASA data with the keys we hold, within hours, reliably?), demonstration impact 0.25 (does the demo look and feel extraordinary in a 3-minute judging video?), AI leverage 0.20 (redefined: how much genuine intelligence value can we add given that chat-LLMs cannot be called at runtime — pushing us toward on-device models, rule engines and precomputed analytics), narrative strength 0.15 (does the story move non-specialist judges?), and competition density 0.10 (how many teams historically swarm this challenge type, and how strong is the median entry?).

Three ground rules kept the matrix honest. First, every score cites a verifiable fact — a tested endpoint, a documented dataset, a historical participation pattern — not a vibe. Second, we penalized challenges whose winning bars are set by domains where we hold no edge (native mobile games, youth-oriented art). Third, we rewarded challenges where our specific credentials create unfair advantage: the FIRMS MAP_KEY, the elevated 10,000 req/h api.nasa.gov key, Earthdata Login JWT for CMR/ASF, GIBS raster+vector tiles, and a full-access Hugging Face token.

The decisive constraint set

  • API-first, chat-only AI: no runtime chat-LLM calls, no local GPU inference — intelligence must come from on-device HF models, deterministic rule engines, or precomputed pipelines.
  • Automation-driven build: the implementation itself is executed through an API/MCP-driven workflow (documented in the companion runbook), so challenges with clean, scriptable data paths are strongly preferred.
  • Deployment must be free and instant: serverless-friendly architecture (Next.js API routes) with zero paid dependencies.
Scoring was performed after live verification of every credential: FIRMS mapkey_status, api.nasa.gov X-RateLimit headers (10,000 req/h — ten times the anonymous tier), GIBS WMTS raster + vector tiles (full FIRMS-schema fire points back to November 2000), CMR acceptance of the Earthdata JWT, and Hugging Face identity. The matrix therefore measures reality, not assumptions.

03Winner Deep-Dive — Challenge 09

"Harmonization of MODIS and VIIRS Hot Spots" — build a unified, 20+ year calendar of fire activity and an early-warning capability.

The challenge asks teams to reconcile two generations of NASA's active-fire record: MODIS (Terra 2000, Aqua 2002; 1 km pixels, two overpasses daily) and VIIRS (S-NPP 2012, NOAA-20 2018, NOAA-21 2024; 375 m pixels, wider swath, far finer fire discrimination). Raw detection counts are not comparable across the sensor break — VIIRS routinely detects several times more fires for the same landscape because it resolves smaller, cooler burns. The deliverable is a web application presenting a harmonized "calendar of fire activity" across 20+ years, plus an early-warning component, using authentic NASA fire data.

Why it is the highest-probability win

  • Credential-perfect fit (9.8/10 data). Our FIRMS MAP_KEY queries the area/count APIs directly; our elevated NASA key covers EONET and imagery services; Earthdata Login opens CMR/ASF; GIBS serves both true-color imagery and — decisively — vector tiles of FIRMS-derived thermal anomalies with the complete per-pixel schema (latitude, longitude, brightness, FRP, confidence, day/night, satellite) back to 2000-11-01. Every layer of the required stack is pre-authorized.
  • Scientific depth that judges can verify. We compute a measured continuity coefficient (VIIRS/MODIS detection ratio over 155–174 overlap months per region — 12.1× to 29.2× in our live build) and use it to fuse records into one 25-year series. This is exactly the "harmonization" intellectual work the challenge rewards.
  • Demo theatre with substance (8.6/10). A globe of yesterday's fires sized by fire radiative power, a quarter-century calendar heatmap, a seasonal outlook, and a live alert level — four visual registers in one screen, all driven by real data.
  • Resilient under sandbox limits. When FIRMS is firewall-blocked (as in our build sandbox), the app transparently falls back to GIBS vector tiles — the same underlying detections — so the demo can never die on stage.
Live-build evidence: the winning app IGNIS exists today — 8 regional calendars (25 years × 12 months × 3 sensors), harmonization ratios per region, 12-month seasonal outlooks with uncertainty bands, EONET early-warning ranked by an on-device Hugging Face MiniLM model, and 758 real MODIS detections rendered for yesterday's Amazon. 04The Two Chosen Challengers

Why 02 and 06 earned the #2 and #3 slots — and what we would build if the primary were unavailable.

#02 — Be An Earth System Trend Detective 7.70

Essence: pick NASA-measured Earth variables or model products, determine whether genuine trends exist over time, and visualize their statistical significance.

Why it ranks #2: the data path is equally credential-covered (GIBS L3 monthly products, POWER, CMR); the intellectual core — trend significance — matches our statistics tooling; and the same automation pipeline (precompute + static hosting) applies. Its harmonized-record logic even shares code with IGNIS.

Why not #1: the demo reads as charts, not spectacle; judging rewards visual narrative, and charts-heavy entries have a historically lower Global Awards hit rate. It remains our designated pivot if FIRMS-class data ever became unavailable.

#06 — Dancing with the SARs 7.83

Essence: use NISAR's L-band/S-band synthetic aperture radar to track surface change — wetlands, fires, quakes, agriculture, ice.

Why it ranks #3: the story is the strongest in the list (a brand-new radar eye on Earth, open data, disaster relevance), and our Earthdata JWT already authenticates ASF downloads. SAR change-detection pairs beautifully with fire calendars (burn-scar verification).

Why not higher: SAR granule processing (calibration, speckle, geocoding) inside a hackathon window is the heaviest lift in the shortlist, and 2026 product coverage for time-series depth is still maturing — an unforgivable schedule risk for a winning target.

Portfolio logic

We commit 100% of build effort to Challenge 09 and hold 02/06 as documented pivots with pre-verified data paths. This is deliberate: Space Apps judges reward depth over breadth, and the winning entry must feel inevitable rather than assembled. The two challengers still shape IGNIS — the trend-detective lens informs our significance testing on seasonal peaks, and the SAR relationship gives the pitch a forward-looking closing line ("tomorrow, NISAR verifies every scar this calendar remembers").

Second-order advantage: all three challenges share one substrate — Earthdata Cloud credentials, GIBS/CMR access and harmonization analytics. Our investment is portable: a win on 09 funds credibility; a pivot costs days, not weeks.

What we consciously rejected

The two game challenges (03, 13) were rejected on competition grounds — they attract specialist game-jam teams and reward art pipelines we do not have. The Jukebox (14) and Abandoned Hardware (01) reward audio/editorial craft with narrow engineering headroom. CLPS (04) and SPHEREx (12) are scientifically delightful but hinge on physics or astronomy domain depth where a single subtle error costs credibility. Health Monitoring (05) collides with medical-claim sensitivity — the hardest category to make valid in one weekend. Every rejection is a risk decision, not a quality judgement.
