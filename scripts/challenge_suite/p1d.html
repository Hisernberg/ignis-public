#04 CLPS Lunar Mission Browser ADV+INTEXPLORATION ◆ MOON ◆ SOFTWARE
An intuitive tool for comparing lunar south-pole landing sites and dates by visualizing Sun and Earth positions relative to the horizon — assessing solar power potential and direct-to-Earth communication windows without wrestling complicated interfaces. The physics is deterministic and free: JPL Horizons ephemerides through Skyfield or SPICE compute sun/earth elevation angles for any site and minute, and the science anchors are published (south-pole illumination averages 77–88 percent, peaking on the Shackleton–de Gerlache ridge). Timing is perfect — CLPS landers including Firefly’s Blue Ghost Mission 4 are slated for the south pole. Scores A− not A because the demo is curve-plots rather than spectacle, and AI leverage is naturally thin. Bench reserve with the cleanest math on the slate; the low-competition lane (9/10) is real. #08 Flame in Freefall: AI-Powered Combustion Insights ADV+INTSPACE EXPLORATION
An interactive, AI-powered dashboard that summarizes, ranks, and interprets NASA’s microgravity combustion experiments for fire-safety insights on the road to Moon and Mars. This is the most AI-native brief on the slate — the product is retrieval, ranking, and explanation, exactly what our LLM + embeddings stack does. The data source is verified: NASA’s Physical Sciences Informatics (PSI) repository is public and now ships a Microgravity Analytics Tool (Nov 2025), with decades of reduced-gravity combustion experiments plus the NTRS technical-report corpus for RAG. The grind: heterogeneous formats, per-experiment structures, and thin standardization mean heavy Saturday data-wrangling. The judges’ science-validity check is also harder to satisfy without a combustionist on the team. Best pure “Best Use of Technology” vehicle on the board; bench reserve.

#11 Interplanetary Survival Guide: Martian Map INTERMEDIATEMARS ◆ SOFTWARE
A layered, integrated view of a Martian location or route that pulls together data from multiple NASA missions and helps a human explorer plan and execute a Marswalk. The stack match is perfect — MapLibre/deck.gl with Mars Trek & PTM tile services, HiRISE/CTX imagery, HRSC topography, MOLA elevation profiles, thermal and dust opacity overlays — and the demo (a guided Marswalk with elevation profiles and hazard layers) lands well. The problem is the field: Mars maps are the single most-built artifact in Space Apps history, the Intermediate flag invites hundreds of polished-but-shallow entries, and differentiation shifts entirely to data depth and narrative polish. A B+ that would need a pre-built Mars tile pipeline to jump tiers. Not selected; revisit only if a second squad forms with spare map capacity.

#12 Planet X and SPHEREx ADVANCEDASTROPHYSICS ◆ SOFTWARE
A public web tool displaying SPHEREx all-sky images so anyone can see how the sky changes over time — the blink-comparator that found Pluto, rebuilt for a 102-band infrared survey that maps the whole sky every six months. IRSA began weekly SPHEREx releases in July 2025, so data is public and current; the mission’s novelty guarantees relevance. The mechanics (position shifts between epochs, difference imaging, cutout services) are well-documented astronomy. The barriers: image physics is unforgiving — scale, dithering, band selection — and a beautiful product demands heavy optimization for petabyte-adjacent archives. AI leverage is modest (catalog cross-matching more than model inference). For an astro-fluent team this is a gem with a 9/10 open field; for ours it is a B−. Skipped, with respect.

#01 Abandoned but not Forgotten INT+YOUTHASTROPHYSICS ◆ STORYTELLING
Tell the story of NASA’s discarded hardware on the Moon, Mars, and deep space — rovers, instruments, probes — in a way that introduces school-age enthusiasts to the hardware and the science it made possible. The strongest narrative brief in years: Apollo descent stages, buried rovers, Voyager’s still-whispering signal. An LLM-guided interactive archive with a 3D or map timeline would score 10/10 on story and decent AI leverage (retrieval + narration). Two structural caps: the data is archival imagery and text rather than live APIs (DATA 6), and the youth flag predicts the largest, warmest field on the slate (COMP 4) — meaning the video must be extraordinary to rise. This is the pick if the team mutates to include a filmmaker; with our profile, it is a B.

#03 Build a Junior Astronaut Mission Trainer INT+YOUTHGAMES ◆ MOONS ◆ STEM
An interactive game where students run a lunar or Martian outpost, balancing life support, radiation shielding, power, and food production — real engineering trade-offs made tangible. The systems-design is genuinely fun for a full-stack team: a simulation loop with resource stocks/flows, event cards, and an LLM mission-advisor that explains consequences in kid language. But game briefs are won on game-feel — juice, art, pacing — and judged against hundreds of student-built entries whose charm is unquantifiable. Competition density scores 3/10 (largest field), and polish requirements eat the 48 hours. The science content also risks oversimplification, the exact failure the brief warns against. Skipped: our edge is data and maps, not game art.

#13 Space Mission Design Game INT+YOUTHGAMES
Design, manage, and simulate a complete space mission — objectives, spacecraft, instruments, launch vehicles, budgets, power, mass, comms, orbital constraints — as an interactive game. The education value is high and the trade-space (mass vs. power vs. cost vs. science return) is rich enough for a serious simulator with real orbital mechanics. The same verdict as #03 applies with interest: a credible mission simulator is a multi-week game-dev project to reach the polish bar, the field is youth-heavy and crowded, and our pre-built assets contribute almost nothing. If the team ever splits and the second squad contains a game developer, this and #03 become their shortlist — not ours. C tier.

#05 Create Health Monitoring Software for Astronauts INTERMEDIATEHUMAN EXPLORATION
Build software that gathers health indicators and lets astronauts evaluate and act on their own status across radiation, isolation, altered gravity, bone loss, and behavioral health on long missions. The mission matters and the self-monitoring angle is fresh. The scoring problem is data: authentic biomedical datasets are sparse, gated behind accessible-but-locked archives (NASA OpenScience Life Sciences, HLSR), and every credible indicator needs validated baselines a hackathon team cannot generate. The demo risks looking like a dashboard of invented numbers — the single fastest way to fail the Validity criterion. A well-scoped version (checklist + trend tracking against published thresholds, privacy-first) can medal locally, but the global ceiling is low without real data partnerships. C tier; skipped.
