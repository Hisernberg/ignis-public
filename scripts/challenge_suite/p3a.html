Mission Briefing

Why this challenge is armed second, and what this document commands.

PULSE is the pivot that keeps the portfolio honest: if Challenge #09 saturates, if FIRMS misbehaves on event weekend, or if the team decides the science lane is the stronger trophy to chase, PULSE is already armed. It addresses Be An Earth System Trend Detective! — find variables measured by NASA missions or produced by NASA models, visualize how they change over time, and determine what is changing, where, how much, and whether the change is statistically significant. The brief’s sharpest edge is its insistence on regional contrast: a variable can rise in one region while falling in another under the same global process, and the app that shows that honestly is doing real science communication.

This plan is deliberately shorter than the flagship’s because it should be: roughly seventy percent of the engine — Next.js shell, MapLibre/deck.gl stack, ingestion pipelines, time-series store, statistics engine, Chronos-Bolt service, LLM briefing service — is specified once in SP-2026-003 and reused here under different skin. What is unique to PULSE is the significance methodology, the variable catalog, and the “opposite trends” story engine. Those are specified fully here. Sections mirror the flagship document: decoded brief (01), significance science (02), variable catalog (03), product (04), shared engine (05), lanes and timeline (06), battle plan and pitch (07–08), risks (09).

PIVOT DOCTRINE
PULSE is not a consolation build. It is the same weapon aimed at a different trophy — Best Use of Science — with a methodology page that referees can verify line by line. Commit only if Gate A data checks pass and the flagship is genuinely blocked; otherwise it remains the war reserve.
SECTION 01 // THE BRIEF, DECODED
What the Challenge Actually Demands

Four deliverable verbs hide inside one advanced-tier sentence.

The brief decomposes into four obligations. Find: select variables from NASA missions or model outputs — the size of this universe is the trap; undisciplined teams drown. PULSE ships a curated catalog (Section 03) of six variable families with verified access paths, chosen for data continuity, spatial completeness, and trend interpretability. Visualize: show how each variable changes over time — not just a global average line, but spatially resolved trend maps where every pixel or region carries its own story. Quantify: how much — magnitude per decade, not vibes; Sen’s slope with units. And judge significance: the Mann-Kendall test with proper handling of autocorrelation, reported honestly, including where trends are not significant — the restraint that makes the significant findings believable.

The judging read is equally clear. This is the only challenge flagged Advanced-only on the Earth side, which predicts a smaller, more serious field and judges who will open the methodology tab. The phrase “what scientists call significant” is an invitation to name our methods formally — doing so on the page and in the pitch is cheap credibility. The regional-contrast clause is the creativity hook: PULSE’s signature artifact is the Contrast Story, a two-panel view that finds and narrates places where the same variable trends in opposite directions. That single screen answers the brief’s hardest clause with a demo moment judges remember.
