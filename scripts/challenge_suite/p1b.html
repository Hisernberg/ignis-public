SECTION 02 // THE TARGETING SYSTEM
Five Dimensions, One Firing Solution

The scoring matrix that converts fourteen briefs into a ranked list — and why each weight is what it is.

Challenge selection fails when it is done by enthusiasm. Teams fall in love with a topic, discover on Saturday night that the data is gated or the science is out of reach, and ship a demo that works only on slides. The matrix below forces the opposite behavior: every candidate is scored 0–10 on five independent dimensions, each anchored to verifiable evidence, and combined by fixed weights into a single weighted composite. The weights encode one belief: at Space Apps, data plumbing kills more projects than imagination does. A beautiful app with no data is a screensaver; a modest app with a relentless data feed is a weapon.

02.1 How Each Dimension Is Evidenced
  • Data availability (0.30). Verified before scoring: archive depth, spatial resolution, latency, API quotas, and registration friction. Example: FIRMS distributes MODIS thermal anomalies back to November 2000 and VIIRS 375 m detections back to 20 January 2012, free, no key walls — an easy 10/10. A brief requiring on-demand SAR processing of terabyte pairs scores far lower on feasibility even when data is public.
  • Demo wow (0.25). Judged from the judge’s chair: does the screen animate, do patterns emerge unbidden, does the video open on something moving? InSAR deformation maps score 9; a well-designed health checklist scores 5.
  • AI leverage (0.20). The playbook’s Hugging Face division — Chronos-Bolt, Prithvi-EO-2.0, SAM, MiniLM, ClimateBERT — must do load-bearing work: forecasting, segmentation, retrieval, briefing. Challenges where an LLM is the product score highest.
  • Story power (0.15). Wildfires, food security, and stranded hardware on the Moon carry their own headlines. Abstract variable analysis does not — unless the app manufactures the story through contrast and significance verdicts.
  • Competition density (0.10). Youth-flagged briefs and game briefs attract the largest fields. Niche, math-heavy briefs with intimidating interfaces attract the smallest. Low competition does not win alone — which is why it carries the lightest weight — but it changes what “excellent” means in a field.

Two adjustments sit on top of the raw composite. First, a stack-fit tiebreak: when two challenges score within 0.15 of each other, the pick goes to the one that reuses the pre-built TERRA SENTINEL chassis — Next.js 15 shell, MapLibre/deck.gl maps, Python ingestion pipelines, FastAPI micro-services, and the HF model registry — because pre-built percentage is the true currency of a 48-hour event. Second, an award-lane check: the final portfolio must attack distinct Global Award categories rather than three challenges that all compete for the same trophy. The verdict in Section 06 applies both adjustments explicitly.

A note on what the matrix is not: it is not a claim that the bottom tiers are weak challenges. #01 is the best pure storytelling brief NASA has issued in years, and #14 might produce the most delightful 90-second video of the event. The matrix scores winnability by this team — a two-to-four-person full-stack unit with a pre-built Earth-observation platform and an AI registry — against a judging funnel that eliminates 96 percent of all projects before global finals. Chasing the best story with the wrong toolkit is how 11,000 other projects die.
